# Cauca (department of Colombia)

## Summary

Cauca is a department in southwestern Colombia, on the Pacific slope of the Andes, bordering Ecuador's Nariño frontier zone in the north-adjacent sense (it borders Nariño department to the south), with its capital at Popayán. This entry captures Cauca as a first-order administrative division of Colombia from 1886 — the year the country adopted the unitary constitution that replaced the federal Estados Unidos de Colombia and fixed departments (rather than sovereign states) as the standing subnational tier — through the present day. `type: subnational` because Colombia held sovereignty throughout; the entry exists to carry departmental-level reporting territory, not statehood. The 1886 start follows this country's default start rule (the system floor for departments that predate the 1915-era data and are not among the country's listed 1991-constitution departures such as Amazonas, Arauca, Casanare, Guainía, Guaviare, Putumayo or San Andrés) rather than the 1915 date at which the routed data itself begins. Cauca is not one of those 1991 departures, and no existing COL-* row was a department-level match, so it required a new entry rather than reuse of a national total.

**Why this entry exists.** The routed input is Mitchell-family or comparable department-level statistics for "Cauca" spanning 1915 onward, previously left unmatched or folded into a national COL-* total (COL-1922-2025 or its predecessors), which over-covers by roughly an order of magnitude since those rows describe all of Colombia rather than one southwestern department. Cauca is a real, longstanding department — one of the units constituted under the 1886 unitary system, tracing back further to the Estado Soberano del Cauca of the earlier federal period — and is not among this country's list of departments carved out under the 1991 constitution, which is why the 1886 system floor applies instead of a later creation date or the 1915 data start. Historical administrative sources (Colombian departmental gazetteers and the DANE administrative-division record) confirm Cauca as a continuously distinct department distinguishable from its various territorial offshoots (Valle del Cauca separated 1910, Huila 1905, and parts ceded toward Caquetá and Putumayo), which is why it needs its own row rather than being absorbed into a neighboring department's polygon or the national total.

## Territorial extent

**Polygon:** Not yet assigned. No polygon available in the GeoPackage for this period. The correct registered source is `gadm-4.1-adm1`, which normally publishes Colombian department-level admin-1 boundaries, but the GADM 4.1 data currently staged locally is a curated 81-country subset that does not include Colombia at all (0 Colombian features present). This is a registered-source-unfetched situation, not a missing-source situation: GADM 4.1 adm1 needs to be (re-)fetched with Colombia included before a feature id (something like `COL.12_1`, whichever GID_1 corresponds to Cauca) can be assigned.

**Territory description:** modern Cauca department covers roughly 29,300 km2 in southwestern Colombia, stretching from the Pacific coastal lowlands (around Guapi and Timbiquí) up through the Cauca valley highlands to Popayán and the páramo country toward the Colombian Massif, bordering Valle del Cauca to the north, Huila and Caquetá to the east, Putumayo and Nariño to the south, and the Pacific Ocean to the west. This is the approximate present-day extent; no polygon is yet attached, so this figure is territory evidence from external gazetteers, not a measurement of any attached geometry. The department's boundaries have shifted since 1886 as Valle del Cauca (1910) and Huila (1905) were carved off and as the Amazonian frontier toward Caquetá/Putumayo was progressively organized as separate territories, so a modern GADM boundary applied across the full 1886-2025 span is an approximation that overstates precision for the earlier decades.

## Sourced claims

- Colombia's 1886 constitution replaced the federal Estados Unidos de Colombia with a unitary republic and converted the former sovereign states, including the Estado Soberano del Cauca, into departments — a change datable in Colombian constitutional histories and DANE's own administrative-division timeline.
- Valle del Cauca was separated from Cauca department in 1910 and Huila in 1905, both documented in Colombian departmental-creation records (e.g. DANE's división político-administrativa histories), meaning any single polygon applied to the full 1886-2025 span understates Cauca's earlier extent.

### d-code-choice-cau

**Chose COL-CAU-1886-2025 following the dominant ISO3-SUBUNIT pattern**

57 of 66 subnational rows in the polity table use <ISO3>-<SUBUNIT>-<start>-<end>, and Cauca is an ordinary standing department with no bespoke historical name of its own (unlike Alaska Territory or Hyderabad), so the bespoke-code exception does not apply. CAU is the ISO 3166-2:CO subdivision code for Cauca, matching the convention seen in ESP-CA, ESP-AS, etc. No existing COL subnational code exists yet, so this entry sets the precedent for the country rather than following one.

### oq-cauca-boundary-vintage

**No polygon is attached, and even once GADM 4.1 is refetched with Colombia included, the modern boundary will misrepresent the 1886-1910 territory**

Cauca lost Huila in 1905 and Valle del Cauca in 1910, both while inside this entry's own 1886-2025 span, so a single modern GADM admin-1 polygon applied across the whole span would overstate Cauca's post-1910 extent for roughly its first 24 years. Whether this warrants splitting this entry at 1905 or 1910 into narrower-span sub-rows, or simply flagging the pre-1910 portion of the span with a vintage-drift status once a polygon is fetched, is unresolved and depends on whether the routed data (from 1915) actually needs pre-1910 territory to be correct.

### oq-cauca-frontier-cessions

**Later cessions toward Caquetá/Putumayo on Cauca's Amazonian frontier are not accounted for**

Beyond the 1905/1910 splits, Cauca's eastern and southern extent toward the Amazonian lowlands was progressively reorganized into separate national territories (ancestors of modern Caquetá and Putumayo departments) over the early-to-mid 20th century. The exact dates and whether any of the routed 1915+ data year-ranges fall on the wrong side of those cessions has not been checked in this pass.
